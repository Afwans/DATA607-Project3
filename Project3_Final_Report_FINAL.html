1 Introduction

1.1 Motivation

Research question: Which data science skills are most valued?

The demand for data science professionals continues to evolve as organizations adopt data analytics, machine learning, and artificial intelligence technologies. Understanding which skills employers request most frequently can help students and job seekers prioritize their professional development. If you are thinking about a career in data science or trying to grow in one, knowing which skills employers actually care about is essential.

“Valued” is not directly observable, so we explore it through two proxies:

Proxy Definition What it captures
Demand Share of data-related postings whose description mentions the skill How often employers ask for it
Pay Difference in advertised salary between postings that do and do not mention the skill, before and after adjusting for job category What employers are willing to pay for it

1.2 Data and Approach

We selected the Kaggle dataset Job Postings with Salaries, which records the jobs posted in the past week on 4,000+ company career sites, with pay ranges. Job postings pair the skills employers ask for with the pay they offer.

We did explore other databases. For instance, there were other sources for CVs (such as here and here) but although CVs could be a good proxy, they reflect what people offer, not what employers are looking for. We also explored descriptions about courses and programs on data science (such as here and here). However, they reflect what these institutions teach, which is not necessarily reasonable proxy. The selected dataset is the best option among these alternatives as it clearly shows what companies expect their workers to do and which skills they are expected to have.

Additionally we looked at the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics which has data on employment trends, job openings and turnover, and employment projections. These data are available for various years and by sector. However, unlike the selected data set, they do not mention skills, just the types of jobs.

Our analysis starts from 9,173 job postings. Our team filtered these down to 226 postings that were clearly data science related (an initial sample of 229 was reduced to 226 after manual review removed three unrelated postings), extracted 30 specific technical skills from each job description, and organized everything into a clean five-table relational database. The data are stored in a PostgreSQL relational database and analyzed using R. The data acquisition and preparation pipeline lives in scripts 01–13 and is documented step by step in DATA607_Project3_Dataset1_Documentation.Rmd.

The project also includes an interactive Shiny dashboard for exploring job market patterns, salaries, and technical skill requirements.

4 Database Design and Data Storage

4.1 Database Management System

We used PostgreSQL as our relational database management system and pgAdmin 4 to manage and query the database.

Our database was named data607_project3.

We organized the job posting data into separate relational tables instead of keeping all information in one large table. This approach reduces unnecessary duplication, improves data organization, and makes it easier to analyze relationships between jobs, companies, salaries, and skills.

4.2 Database Tables

The full database used for the dashboard contains seven tables covering all 9,173 postings:

4.3 Database Relationships

We used unique identifiers to connect related tables.

  • Company and Job Posting: Each job posting is associated with a company through company_id.
  • Job Posting and Salary: Salary information is connected to a job through job_id.
  • Job Posting and Skills: The job_skill table connects jobs and skills through job_id and skill_id.
  • Data Science Classification: Identified Data Science jobs are linked to their original postings through job_id.
  • Job Sector Classification: Each posting is assigned a broader employment sector using job_id.

The job_skill table represents a many-to-many relationship because one job may require multiple skills, and the same skill may appear in multiple jobs.

The relationships are reflected in the ER Diagram.

4.4 Database Normalization

Our relational design follows normalization principles by separating different types of information into related tables.

For example, company information is stored in the company table rather than repeated in every job posting. Similarly, skill names are stored in the skill table, while job_skill records the relationship between skills and postings.

This structure reduces repeated information and supports efficient SQL joins.

4.5 Data Import Process

After downloading and extracting the Kaggle dataset, we imported the data into our PostgreSQL database using pgAdmin 4.

We followed these steps:

  1. Created a PostgreSQL database named data607_project3.
  2. Created separate relational tables to organize the dataset.
  3. Defined the required columns and data types for each table.
  4. Imported the extracted data files into their corresponding tables.
  5. Connected the tables using identifiers such as job_id, company_id, and skill_id.
  6. Used SQL queries to verify that the data was imported correctly.

After importing the data, we checked the record counts and relationships between tables.

This process allowed us to organize the original dataset into a relational structure that supports SQL queries and further analysis in R.

We examined missing information, particularly salary fields and job attributes.

We retained the complete dataset for general job market analysis while using only eligible records for salary calculations.

6 Value Proxy 1: Demand

To identify which Data Science skills are most valued by employers, we first measured how frequently each skill appeared in Data Science job postings. If a skill shows up in 70% of postings, that is a strong signal that it is widely expected, almost a requirement just to be considered for most roles.

We defined skill demand as the percentage of Data Science postings mentioning a particular skill. We searched all 226 job descriptions for 30 specific skills using keyword rules.

Because a single job posting may request multiple skills, the percentages do not necessarily add up to 100%.

6.3 Demand differs by role

So far we have been looking at all 226 postings together. But data science is not one job; it is a family of different roles. A Data Analyst and an AI/ML Engineer both work with data, but they do very different things and earn very different salaries. If we lump them together, we might get misleading results.

This section breaks the data down by job category to answer:

  1. Does each category have its own distinct set of top skills, or do they all need the same things?
  2. Is Python truly universal, or is it just concentrated in a few big categories?

8 Value Proxy 2: Pay

Skill demand is one way to measure how much employers value a particular skill. However, frequently requested skills are not necessarily associated with the highest salaries. A skill that shows up everywhere might just be a basic expectation, something every employer assumes you have, so it does not necessarily push your salary higher.

We restricted the salary analysis to postings with eligible annual salary estimates in USD. This avoids mixing different currencies or including records without comparable salary information. We used the median instead of relying only on the mean because unusually high salaries can influence averages.

8.4 Skill salary premiums

To find skills that are truly valued, we also look at whether having a skill is associated with higher pay. This section compares the median salary of jobs that mention a skill against the median salary of jobs that do not mention that skill. The difference is what we call the salary premium: a positive number means that skill tends to appear in higher paying jobs; a negative number means the opposite.

  • Skill premium: median salary of postings that mention the skill minus the median of postings that do not, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval.

Important note: This is a description of patterns in the data, not proof that learning a skill will raise your salary. A job requiring PyTorch might already be a senior level role that pays well for many reasons; the skill mention and the high salary are both symptoms of the same thing, not cause and effect.

boot_median_diff <- function(y, x, reps = 2000) {
  est <- median(y[x]) - median(y[!x])
  b <- replicate(reps, {
    i <- sample(length(y), replace = TRUE)
    yy <- y[i]; xx <- x[i]
    median(yy[xx]) - median(yy[!xx])
  })
  tibble(raw = est, raw_lo = quantile(b, 0.025, na.rm = TRUE),
         raw_hi = quantile(b, 0.975, na.rm = TRUE))
}

premiums <- map_dfr(eligible, function(s) {
  d <- pay_model %>% mutate(has = job_id %in% pay_skills$job_id[pay_skills$skill_name == s])
  fit <- lm(log_salary ~ has + cat_model, data = d)
  co  <- tidy(fit, conf.int = TRUE) %>% filter(term == "hasTRUE")
  bind_cols(
    tibble(skill_name = s, n_with = sum(d$has)),
    boot_median_diff(d$salary_midpoint_annual, d$has),
    tibble(adj = exp(co$estimate) - 1, adj_lo = exp(co$conf.low) - 1,
           adj_hi = exp(co$conf.high) - 1, adj_p = co$p.value)
  )
}) %>%
  left_join(select(demand, skill_name,share), by = "skill_name") %>%
  arrange(desc(adj))

9 Conclusion: Which Skills Are Truly Most Valued?

We have looked at two separate things: how often skills are demanded (Section 6) and how much salary is associated with them (Section 8). Now we put both together into a single Value Score that ranks each skill by combining both dimensions.

The logic is simple: a skill scores highest when it is both frequently requested AND linked to higher pay. A skill that is only common but pays no premium gets penalized. A skill that pays well but is only requested in 2% of jobs also gets penalized.

The lower the Value Score, the more valued the skill is overall.

9.1 Discussion

We examined 9,173 job postings, including 226 Data Science positions, using data obtained from Kaggle. We evaluated skill value using two measures: how frequently employers requested a skill and the advertised salaries associated with jobs requiring that skill.

Our analysis found that Python and SQL were the most frequently requested skills among Data Science postings. Python appeared in 163 postings (72.1%), while SQL appeared in 133 postings (58.8%). Machine Learning, Large Language Models (LLMs), and Statistics were also commonly requested.

When examining advertised salaries, specialized skills such as Generative AI, PyTorch, Deep Learning, and LLMs were associated with some of the highest median salaries.

We also found that Data Science positions had a median advertised annual salary of approximately $165,000, compared with $110,000 across the overall job market.

There is no single “most valued” skill. The proxies (demand, pay) agree on some skills and disagree on others. We’ve split the skills into four tiers:

Tier 3 — Specialist Premium PyTorch, Deep Learning, Generative AI Less common but linked to the largest salary premiums

11 Limitations

Although our analysis provides useful insights into Data Science skill demand and advertised salaries, several limitations should be considered.

11.1 Limited Time Period

Our dataset contains job postings dated October 5 through October 12, 2026. Because this represents a short period, the findings may not reflect long-term hiring trends or seasonal changes in employer demand. Although the Kaggle dataset is updated weekly, our analysis uses one fixed snapshot, not a comprehensive market survey. Future work could download the dataset weekly and track trends in the job market longitudinally.

11.2 Representativeness

The postings come from companies that use specific applicant-tracking systems and are concentrated in the US and in tech.

11.3 Data Science Sample Size

Of the 9,173 job postings, only 226 were classified as Data Science positions. Furthermore, only 178 Data Science postings had eligible annual salary estimates in USD. This relatively small sample limits how broadly we can generalize the findings. The 48 postings without salary data could have different skill patterns that we cannot account for here.

11.4 Salary Information

Not every job posting included salary information suitable for comparison. We restricted salary analysis to eligible annual USD salary estimates and excluded other currencies. As a result, salary findings do not represent every posting in the dataset.

Advertised salaries may also differ from actual compensation, bonuses, and benefits.

11.5 Skill Identification

Our analysis depends on the skills identified in the job posting data. Skills were detected using keyword matching in job descriptions, which cannot distinguish required from preferred skills or detect proficiency. A posting might mention Python as a nice-to-have rather than a requirement.

Some employers may require skills without explicitly mentioning them in their postings. Additionally, a skill appearing in a posting does not indicate how extensively it will be used.

11.6 Factors Affecting Salary

Salary differences may reflect factors beyond technical skills, including:

  • Job seniority and experience requirements (our models do not take seniority into account).
  • Geographic location.
  • Employer and industry.
  • Job responsibilities and specialization.

Therefore, the relationship between a skill and advertised salary should not be interpreted as causal. Senior roles pay more for many reasons, and they also tend to require advanced skills.

11.7 No Supply and Demand Comparison

We did not integrate CV or resume data, so we cannot compare the skills employers ask for with the skills candidates offer.

12 Future Work

Our project could be expanded in several ways.

  1. Longitudinal analysis: Collect weekly Kaggle dataset updates to examine changes in skill demand over time.

  2. Geographic pay comparisons: Compare skill demand and advertised salaries across different locations.

  3. Geographic company comparisons: Companies, given their business models, need specific skills and companies with similar characteristics may be located in geographic proximity to each other.

  4. Experience-level analysis: Examine how salary associations differ between entry-level, mid-level, and senior positions.

  5. Skill combinations: Investigate whether combinations such as Python, SQL, and Machine Learning are associated with different salary patterns.

  6. Additional data sources: Compare the Kaggle dataset with other job posting datasets to evaluate how consistent the findings are, and integrate CV or resume data to compare supply with demand.

  7. Advanced statistical analysis: Explore statistical models that account for factors such as location, seniority, and industry.

  8. Job sector analysis: Examine the skill trends for data science jobs in different industries/sectors.

These improvements could provide a more comprehensive understanding of Data Science skill demand and compensation.

12.1 Key Takeaways

1. Python is the clearest answer to the question. It is the most demanded skill at 72% of postings, it appears across every job category, and it is associated with above-average salaries. If you only learn one skill, make it Python.

2. SQL is still essential, even in 2026. Despite being considered a “basic” skill, SQL appeared in nearly 60% of postings and is present in every job type.

3. AI and ML skills are where the money is. Generative AI, PyTorch, Deep Learning, and LLMs appear in fewer postings than Python or SQL, but they are consistently linked to the highest-paying roles.

4. Skills travel in bundles. The job market does not value skills in isolation. Python + SQL + Machine Learning is a classic core stack, and most employers who want one expect the others too.

5. The type of job matters as much as the skills. Data Science and AI/ML roles pay roughly $75,000 more per year than Data Analytics and Business Intelligence roles. Before investing in new skills, consider which type of role you are targeting; the category premium is often larger than the skill premium.

Overall, our findings suggest that a combination of foundational programming, database knowledge, and specialized technical skills may be valuable for individuals pursuing Data Science careers. However, these conclusions are based on a limited dataset snapshot and should not be interpreted as universal job market rankings.
